Cellular adaptation to cancer therapy along 
a resistance continuum
Gustavo S. França1,2, Maayan Baron1,12, Benjamin R. King2,9,12, Jozef P. Bossowski3,12, 
Alicia Bjornberg4,12, Maayan Pour1,2,12, Anjali Rao1,12, Ayushi S. Patel1,2,12, Selim Misirlioglu5, 
Dalia Barkley1, Kwan Ho Tang5,10, Igor Dolgalev6, Deborah A. Liberman1,2, Gal Avital1, 
Felicia Kuperwaser1,2, Marta Chiodin1, Douglas A. Levine5,11, Thales Papagiannakopoulos5,9, 
Andriy Marusyk4, Timothée Lionnet2,7,13 & Itai Yanai1,2,5,8,13 ✉
Advancements in precision oncology over the past decades have led to new 
therapeutic interventions, but the efficacy of such treatments is generally limited by 
an adaptive process that fosters drug resistance1. In addition to genetic mutations2, 
recent research has identified a role for non-genetic plasticity in transient drug 
tolerance3 and the acquisition of stable resistance4,5. However, the dynamics of 
cell-state transitions that occur in the adaptation to cancer therapies remain unknown 
and require a systems-level longitudinal framework. Here we demonstrate that 
resistance develops through trajectories of cell-state transitions accompanied by a 
progressive increase in cell fitness, which we denote as the ‘resistance continuum’. 
This cellular adaptation involves a stepwise assembly of gene expression programmes 
and epigenetically reinforced cell states underpinned by phenotypic plasticity, 
adaptation to stress and metabolic reprogramming. Our results support the notion 
that epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition or stemness programmes—often considered 
a proxy for phenotypic plasticity—enable adaptation, rather than a full resistance 
mechanism. Through systematic genetic perturbations, we identify the acquisition of 
metabolic dependencies, exposing vulnerabilities that can potentially be exploited 
therapeutically. The concept of the resistance continuum highlights the dynamic 
nature of cellular adaptation and calls for complementary therapies directed at the 
mechanisms underlying adaptive cell-state transitions.
The ability of malignant cells to consistently evolve resistance to initially 
effective therapies poses one of the major obstacles to cancer treat-
ment1. Models to explain the emergence of resistance have traditionally 
relied on the selection of genetic mutations enabling cells to escape 
therapy2. More recently, however, the roles of non-genetic mechanisms 
have been increasingly recognized5. In particular, a subset of cancer 
cells can avoid therapeutic elimination by entering a drug-tolerant per-
sister cell state through transcriptional reprogramming3,6,7. Although 
such a drug-tolerant state may be initially reversible, over long-term 
exposure to the drug, heritable adaptive states can eventually impart 
stable resistance8–10.
Recent evidence also suggests that drug resistance unfolds as a 
gradual and multifactorial process11. However, the underlying dynam-
ics and the phenotypic trajectories that mediate drug adaptation 
remain unknown. Here we systematically probe and functionally char-
acterize these trajectories in a series of longitudinal experiments. We 
demonstrate that drug adaptation from initial sensitivity to stable 
resistance can be conceptualized as a series of transitions between 
distinct and progressively adaptive cellular states, which we term the 
‘resistance continuum’.
Drug adaptation along a resistance continuum
To study the dynamics of the adaptive potential of cancer cells, we 
reasoned that long-term dose escalation coupled with single-cell and 
functional tools would provide an experimental framework to reveal 
the gene programmes associated with the emergence of resistance. 
We treated a human BRCA2-deficient high-grade serous ovarian cancer 
(HGSOC) cell line (Kuramochi12) with the PARP-inhibitor (PARPi) olapa-
rib, which induces synthetic lethality in tumours deficient in BRCA1 or 
BRCA213. Drug-naive cells were initially treated with 1 μM of olaparib— 
a relatively low dose—until the population reached confluency, indi-
cating adaptation to the dose. The resulting population was then 
seeded onto a fresh plate and treated with an increased dose of 2.5 μM.

We then repeated this process, each time doubling the drug concentra-
tions, until cells were able to reach confluency at an extreme dose of 
320 μM, thereby generating a panel of 9 adapted cell populations (T1 
to T320) (Fig. 1a). We found that these populations (hereafter ‘lines’) 
became progressively more resistant, with the half maximal inhibi-
tory concentration (IC50) increasing from around 2 μM for the control 
population to around 60 μM for the cells adapted to 320 μM (Fig. 1b). 
This shift indicates an increase in drug tolerance toward higher doses, 
rather than full resistance, as even the most resistant lines continued 
to exhibit sensitivity to the doses to which they had adapted.
To assess the generality of the observed progressive fitness increases, 
we repeated the dose-escalation experiment for two additional 
BRCA-mutant ovarian cancer cell lines, Ovsaho and COV36212, a mela-
noma cell line (A375) treated with dabrafenib (a BRAF inhibitor), and 
a lung adenocarcinoma line (PC9) treated with osimertinib (an EGFR 
inhibitor). We found that the dose-escalation experiments across these 
distinct models and therapies reproducibly generated continuous 
resistant phenotypes (Extended Data Fig. 1a–h).
To test for the possibility that pre-existing fully resistant clones were 
already present in the initial population and selected in the course of our 
assay, we performed two rounds of treatment-recovery experiments on 
drug-naive cells under the highest dose exposure (320 μM). A prediction 
of this scenario is that cells would exhibit increased resistance after the 
first and/or second rounds. However, we observed that the surviving 
cells maintained a sensitive state, suggesting a drug-tolerant persister 
phenotype3 (Extended Data Fig. 2a). We next tested whether the duration 
of the treatment alone in the adapted lines could account for increased 
resistance. We found that prolonged exposure at constant doses con-
tributed to increased resistance but did not elicit similar resistance levels 
to the dose-escalated populations, especially for the less-adapted lines 
(Extended Data Fig. 2b–d). We further tested whether dose escalation 
facilitates adaptation by comparing drug-naive populations exposed 
to acute doses with dose escalation and found that the dose escalation 
resulted in faster adaptation (Extended Data Fig. 2e–h), suggesting that 
lower doses may prime cells to achieve higher resistance.
Overall, these results indicate that gradual drug exposure elicits incre-
mental adaptive responses unfolding along a resistance continuum. We 
therefore hypothesized that this fitness increase is accompanied by a 
series of cellular state transitions occurring along an adaptive path.

across other resistance models revealed similar dynamics of progres-

We next sought to identify the gene expression programmes that 
underpin these adaptive states. Implementing a gene module detec-
tion approach, we defined six modules (Fig. 2a). The modules were 
enriched with genes of distinct functional annotations, many of which 
have been previously identified in cell survival programmes in response 
to therapy15 (Fig. 2b). Module A, expressed by the less-adapted cells, 
included the lineage-defining transcription factors SOX17, PAX8 and 
WT116 as well as epithelial markers (KRT8 and DSP). The expression 
of these genes decreases along the resistance continuum, reflecting 
dedifferentiation (Fig. 2a), which was also observed in the other ovar-
ian cancer models (Extended Data Fig. 3e,j). Module A is also enriched 
for the interferon response (IFI6 and IFI27), an endogenous signature 
that is induced upon DNA damage17 (Fig. 2a,b). Module B genes include 
epithelial (KRT8 and KRT18) and mesenchymal markers (FN1), suggest-
ing a hybrid state. Module C includes genes involved in extracellular 
matrix remodelling (COL3A1 and SERPINE2), epithelial-to-mesenchymal 
transition (EMT) (SOX4 and VIM) and hypoxia (Fig. 2a,b). Module D also 
includes hypoxia genes (CITED2, SGK1 and DUSP1), EMT (SNAI2 and 
VIM), mediators of TGFβ signalling (SMAD3 and DAB2) and stemness 
markers (CD44 and KLF4), suggesting a spectrum of dedifferentiated 
states (Fig. 2a,b and Extended Data Fig. 3e,j). Indeed, we observed 
morphological changes to mesenchymal-like phenotypes early on 
in the adaptive process (Fig. 1a) and found high expression of those 
markers in the resistant populations from the additional ovarian cancer 
models (Extended Data Fig. 3e,j and Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). 
Studying other resistance models confirmed dedifferentiation as a 
general feature of drug adaptation (Extended Data Figs. 3e,j and 4f,l).
The genes assigned to modules E and F reflect metabolic rewiring in 
the highly resistant cells in state S5. These include the hypoxia regula-
tor HIF1A and its target DDIT4, as well as glycolytic enzymes (PFKP and 
GAPDH). Notably, we observed enrichment of NRF2 cytoprotective 
programmes for oxidative stress (TXNRD1, AKR1B1 and GGH) and fer-
roptosis (HMOX1, GPX4 and FTL), which are associated with survival 
advantage in drug-tolerant persisters7. NRF2 regulates the pentose 
phosphate pathway (PPP) (TKT), which contributes to maintaining 
redox balance and provides precursors for de novo nucleotide biosyn-
thesis (IMPDH2) (Fig. 2a,b). A number of ribosomal (RPL14 and RPS15A) 
and mRNA translation genes (EIF1, EIF2D and RACK1) in modules D and 
E suggests translation reprogramming (Supplementary Table 1) con-
trolled by ATF4, a transcription factor that cooperates with NRF2 in the 
integrated stress response18. Genes regulated by NRF2 and ATF4 (ASNS, 
CHAC1 and SHMT2) were consistently detected in the highly resistant 
populations from the additional ovarian, lung and melanoma models 
(Extended Data Figs. 3e,j and 4f,l and Supplementary Tables 2–5).
As the resistance continuum implies that state transitions are caus-
ally related to fitness, we tested whether, within and across adapted 
lines, cells have differential fitnesses according to their states. As our 
gene module definitions identified CD24 and CD44 as markers for 
specific states (Extended Data Fig. 5a), we sorted subpopulations 
from a representative set of lines (C, T10, T40 and T320; Extended 
Data Fig. 5b). Indeed, we found that within the T10 population, state 
S4 cells (CD24lowCD44hi) were more resistant than the state S2 cells 
(CD24lowCD44low; Extended Data Fig. 5c), but were less resistant than 
the CD24lowCD44hi cells from T40 and T320. This indicates that subop-
timal cellular states coexist alongside states of higher fitness within a 
population, whereas fitness increases across the adapted populations.

Lineage plasticity enables drug adaptation
Our transcriptome data identified dedifferentiation as one axis within 
the resistance continuum, as we observed: (1) loss of expression of the 
lineage factor SOX17 (Fig. 2a); (2) upregulation of EMT programmes 
(Fig. 2a,b); and (3) a CD24lowCD44hi phenotype (Extended Data Fig. 5), a 
feature associated with enhanced plasticity and stem-like states19. Thus, 
we aimed to investigate the effect of perturbing these programmes 
on resistance. We induced SOX17 expression in the most adapted line 
(T320) and observed an increase in the sensitivity to olaparib (Extended 
Data Fig. 6a,b). Conversely, decreasing SOX17 expression in the parental 
cells reduced their sensitivity (Extended Data Fig. 6c,d). These exper-
iments suggest that modulating the activity of a lineage factor can 
directly and partially affect resistance levels.
Plasticity and EMT are often associated with ‘stem-like’ states and 
drug resistance19, although the association between these two remains 
poorly understood. One possible model is that the resistance con-
tinuum reflects a progressive increase in EMT or stemness, implying 
a direct explanation for the progressive resistance. Alternatively, EMT 
or stemness may denote the ability of cells to undergo adaptive phe-
notypic reprogramming, although not necessarily directly providing 
high resistance. In this second model, EMT accounts for an essential 
enabling—although not sufficient—property for subsequent state tran-
sitions within the resistance continuum. To distinguish between these 
two scenarios, we first compared the EMT signature scores across our 
resistant populations. We observed an early increase in EMT scores 
within the resistance continuum, yet a progressive increase in resistance 
did not correspond to higher EMT scores (Extended Data Fig. 6e–i). 
Next, we experimentally induced EMT in Kuramochi control cells using 
a lentiviral doxycycline-controlled expression of Slug (SNAI2), a key 
regulator of EMT20. We selected a Slug overexpression level that con-
ferred enhanced invasion resembling that observed in T320 cells in a 
Transwell assay (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 6j–l). We found that 
the Slug-expressing cells were less sensitive to olaparib compared 
with their parental controls, highlighting the role of EMT in providing 
an initial survival advantage. However, these cells were substantially 
less resistant to olaparib compared to the cells on the resistance contin-
uum. After 3 weeks of exposure to 5 μM olaparib, Slug-overexpressing 
cells did transition to net-positive growth, although they were notably 
less resistant than the adapted lines (Fig. 2d). Therefore, these results 
are consistent with the notion that the EMT programme directly and 
partially reduces drug sensitivity, while it enables faster adaptation 
through phenotypic plasticity over longer timescales.
Adapted states are associated with CNAs
We next tested whether the emergence of the adapted cell states is 
associated with copy number alterations (CNAs). We first inferred CNAs 
using the scRNA-seq data and found that alterations in the adapted 
lines largely agreed with our defined state subpopulations (Fig. 3a and 
Extended Data Fig. 7a), indicating clonal genetic evolution. By contrast, 
clearly distinguishable CNAs were not present among the persister 
cells (Extended Data Fig. 7a). We found concordant CNA profiles by 
performing whole-exome sequencing (WES) (Fig. 3a). We observed sig-
nificant overlaps between the genes present in the adaptation modules 
and their gained or deleted status in the CNA regions (Extended Data 
Fig. 7b). Some CNAs were conserved across the adapted lines (regions 
of chromosomes 7 and 9), including a deleted region that spans the 
CDKN2A/B locus (validated by WES), thus suggesting a potential con-
tribution to PARPi resistance. We did not detect secondary BRCA1 or 
BRCA2 mutations in the WES data from the resistant lines.
The association between CNAs and cell states may reflect adaptive 
changes or they may be a consequence of the PARPi treatment, as the 
ensuing replication stress is expected to induce CNAs21. Indeed, we also 
observed that the dose-escalation experiment across other cell lines 
(Ovsaho and COV362) led to cell line-specific CNA profiles (Extended 
Data Fig. 7c), despite exhibiting shared resistance markers (Fig. 2a and 
Extended Data Fig. 3e,j). Additionally, state S5 cells exhibited distinct 
profiles (Fig. 3a, see T160 and T320 cells), suggesting that CNAs alone 
do not completely define an adapted state. This may imply that multiple 
clones evolved in parallel through the resistance continuum.
Progressive chromatin reprogramming
To gain insight into the epigenetic mechanisms underlying drug adap-
tation, we measured genome-wide chromatin accessibility by assay 
for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-seq) in 
the resistant and persister lines. In the adapted lines, we again sorted 
CD24lowCD44hi cells to profile chromatin reprogramming at the sub-
population level (Fig. 3b and Extended Data Fig. 5b). Principal compo-
nent analysis (PCA) on the resulting chromatin accessibility profiles 
revealed that PC1 and PC2 together capture the ancestral order from 
the parental sensitive cells to the T320 adapted cells (Fig. 3c), indicating 
cumulative changes in chromatin accessibility. Chromatin accessibility 
from the T5 to the T320 lines revealed a dynamic process of increas-
ing and shared differentially accessible regions (Fig. 3d). By contrast, 
persister cells exhibited relatively fewer changes (Fig. 3e), mirroring 
the transcriptome findings (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 3k,l).
Enrichment analysis of binding sites for transcription factors pro-
vided additional support for the widespread epigenetic reprogram-
ming (Extended Data Fig. 8a–d). The adapted lines showed a progressive 
decrease in accessibility for the lineage drivers SOX17, PAX8 and WT1 
(Fig. 3e, left), indicating a coordinated dedifferentiation programme. 
Acute drug exposure did not induce the same extent of reprogramming 
in the persister populations. By contrast, the adapted lines exhibited a 
steady increase in accessibility for the global stress regulators AP1, NRF2 
and ATF4 in terms of both the number and proportion of accessible 
peaks (Fig. 3e, right)—suggesting reinforced activity of stress-response 
programmes along the olaparib resistance continuum.
We next explored whether the chromatin changes would be sta-
ble upon drug removal. After growing the most adapted line for 
120 days without drug (T320W), we found that the cells remained 
resistant to lower doses (10 μM and 40 μM) but exhibited pronounced 
sensitivity to 320 μM, implying partial reversibility of the resistance 
(Extended Data Fig. 8e). Notably, we did not observe a tradeoff in 
proliferation, but rather faster rates in T320 and T320W lines rela-
tive to the parental control cells (Extended Data Fig. 8e). Upon drug 
removal and subsequent short re-treatment, less accessible chromatin 
regions remained stable, indicating the maintenance of a dedifferen-
tiated state (Fig. 3f, top and Extended Data Fig. 8f). By contrast, the 
T320W line showed evidence of partial loss of the more accessible 
chromatin regions that had been gained by their parental T320 line. 
Upon re-exposure to the drug, however, the T320W line recovered 
this chromatin accessibility, suggesting an underlying mechanism 
for cellular memory that reinstates stress-associated regulation 
(Fig. 3f, bottom and Extended Data Fig. 8f). Collectively, these find-
ings indicate that drug adaptation involves gradual dedifferentia-
tion alongside dynamic recruitment of reinforced stress-responsive 
regulatory regions.

We next tested whether the adapted and persister states occur in vivo 
using a BRCA2-mutant HGSOC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) 
model. In a first experiment (hereafter, ‘persistence’), we sought to 
study whether treatment with different doses elicits a persister-like

Systems-level metabolic dependencies

programmes in PARPi-induced oxidative stress. We therefore tested 
whether the resistance continuum is coupled with a progressive acquisi-
tion of systems-level metabolic dependencies. To test this hypothesis, 
we performed a CRISPR-based negative-selection screen using a meta-
bolic gene-knockout library24 on Kuramochi drug-naive cells (control

1 μM of olaparib (CD); and the T320 cells on 40 μM of olaparib (T320D)) 
(Fig. 5a and Supplementary Tables 9–14). We observed an increasing 
number of depleted genes between the T10 to T320 when compared to 
the parental control cells, indicating metabolic reprogramming along 
the resistance continuum (Extended Data Fig. 10a,b, Supplementary 
Fig. 9 and Supplementary Tables 15 and 16).

lines T10, T320 and T320D included NRF2 targets involved in redox bal-

tional nucleotide metabolism enzymes (TYMS, CAD and CTPS1) and

antioxidant defence mechanism that produces the reducing co-factor 
NADPH and generates precursors for nucleotide biosynthesis (Fig. 5c). 
Targeted metabolomics confirmed an increased abundance of nucleo-
tides as well as their precursors and derivatives in T320 (Extended Data 
Fig. 10c). Dependencies on cholesterol biosynthesis and oxidative 
phosphorylation were more specific to T320 and T320D, respectively, 
whereas T10 and T320 showed dependencies on iron homeostasis and 
cellular response to starvation pathways (Fig. 5c).
We postulated that cells spanning the resistance continuum would 
gradually become more susceptible to a drug combination targeting 
both the emerging dependencies on NRF2 pathways and nucleotide 
biosynthesis. In lung cancer, hyperactivation of NRF2 pathways leads 
to an increased dependency on glutaminolysis25, thus exposing a vul-
nerability to the glutaminase (GLS) inhibitor CB-839. Ovarian cancer 
cell lines that highly express GLS have also been shown to be more 
sensitive to the combination of CB-839 and olaparib. This drug com-
bination decreases the pool of reduced glutathione (a major antioxi-
dant) and suppresses nucleotide biosynthesis, enhancing replication 
stress26. Therefore, chronic DNA damage caused by continuous olaparib 
treatment may lead to an insufficiency in the nucleotide pool. Upon 
olaparib-induced reactive oxygen species production, adaptation 
through activation of NRF2 antioxidant programmes would alleviate 
reactive oxygen species by regulating glutathione metabolism and PPP, 
which also provides precursors for nucleotide biosynthesis. The GLS 
inhibitor CB-839 depletes glutamate, whose availability is important 
for both glutathione production and nucleotide biosynthesis through 
tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates (Fig. 5d).
Testing the combination of olaparib and CB-839, we indeed found 
that the adapted lines were gradually more vulnerable to the combina-
tion; leading to a sixfold decrease in the IC50 relative to the olaparib- 
only treatment in the most adapted line (Fig. 5e and Extended Data 
Fig. 10d,e). Monotherapy with CB-839 led to only slight sensitivity to 
CB-839, suggesting a synergistic effect with NRF2 activation (Extended 
Data Fig. 10f–h).
Discussion
Our study reveals a process that we term the resistance continuum, 
which involves a gradual adaptation to cancer therapy mediated by 
a series of cell-state transitions, progressively leading to increasing 
fitness (Fig. 5f). In our longitudinal in vitro experiments, we leveraged 
a dose-escalation regime to query the adaptive potential of malig-
nant cells, enabling us to dissect individual stages throughout this 
continuum.
The series of state transitions characterizing a resistance continuum 
encompasses axes of dedifferentiation and physiological adaptation. 
The link between lineage plasticity and partial EMT has been caus-
ally associated with resistance19,27. Here we propose that whereas EMT 
induction directly contributes to partial resistance, it more gener-
ally facilitates phenotypic plasticity over the long term, enabling the 
emergence of additional mechanisms that increase drug resistance.
Our work also provides insight into the underpinning of drug adapta-
tion through the assembly of transcriptional programmes and chro-
matin states, with a steady increase in activity of the stress regulators 
AP1, NRF2 and ATF4. A possible mechanism is that promiscuous AP1 
binding ‘pioneers’ the enhancer landscape, which is potentiated by 
recurrent stress stimuli28,29, thus establishing and maintaining a cellular 
memory upon continuous stress30.
The fitness of a cell population may increase by gradual exposure 
to injury as cells epigenetically ‘learn’ and fine-tune stress regulatory 
networks31, thus priming stable adaptive states. Initially drug-sensitive 
cells thereby accumulate transcriptional and epigenetic changes upon 
encountering stress, leading to more robust responses upon sub­
sequent stimulation. We speculate that a similar process may occur 
during treatment of patients, potentially influenced by drug gradients 
and tumour spatial heterogeneity32. Persister cells, which also rely on 
stress-related programmes7,33 and chromatin remodelling34, may rep-
resent an early stage within the resistance continuum. In this scenario, 
the AP1–NRF2–ATF4 axis may act as a molecular rheostat to support cell 
survival both in acute treatment and throughout the establishment of

cellular memory-based adaptation remain unidentified.
Conceptually, the resistance continuum can incorporate both 
expression-level and mutational changes. Indeed, a combined effect 
of both copy-number and expression-level changes was documented 
in cells that underwent gradual adaptation to different ALK inhibitors11, 
or the acquisition of genetic mechanisms evolved from drug-tolerant 
cells35. Mutational processes, including CNAs, might restrict the epi-
genetic landscapes and accessibility of cell states, thus modifying the 
trajectories of adaptation.
The spectrum of resistance states may be strongly influenced by 
pre-existing variability6,14. As we demonstrate here, extrinsic forces such 
as dosing14 and duration of treatment can shift the adaptive trajectories. 
It is interesting to ponder whether the mechanisms propelling cells 
throughout the resistance continuum are primarily shaped by an evolu-
tionary process, stemming from stochastic events, or are deterministi-
cally constrained by the underlying developmental programmes. The 
emergence of states may be seen as variants emerging from historical 
contingencies36 and their increased fitness fixed through a selective 
regime. Conversely, transitions to attractor states37 induced by therapy 
may exhibit recurrent properties indicative of a learning process. Both 
of these processes are likely to be involved in cellular adaptation and it 
may not be possible to achieve a complete uncoupling.
Finally, we uncovered systems-level dependencies that expose latent 
vulnerabilities along the resistance continuum. This underscores 
the importance of monitoring the emergence of adaptive states in 
clinical practice, as demonstrated in a longitudinal assessment of 
stress-response states enriched during patient chemotherapy38. Given 
the possibility of pharmacological control over cell-state transitions39, 
it might be possible to either impede the adaptive process, or steer the 
evolving populations toward more treatable fates40.

Methods
Cell culture
Kuramochi cells (BRCA2 mutant12) were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Ther-
moFisher) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% MEM non-essential 
amino acids, 1% l-glutamine 200 mM, 1% Antibiotic-Antimycotic solu-
tion 100× and 0.25 U ml−1 insulin solution (Sigma-Aldrich). Ovsaho 
(BRCA2 homozygous deleted12) and PC9 (EGFR exon 19 deletion) 
cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 (ThermoFisher) with 10% FBS, 1% 
l-glutamine 200 mM and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (10,000 U ml−1, 
ThermoFisher). COV362 (BRCA1 mutant12) and A375 (BRAFV600E) cells 
were cultured in DMEM (ThermoFisher) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% 
l-glutamine and 1% penicillin and streptomycin. Cells were maintained 
at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Ovarian cancer cell lines were provided by the 
laboratory of B. Neel. Melanoma A375 cells and lung adenocarcinoma 
(LUAD) PC9 cells were provided by the laboratories of A. Lund and 
K.-K. Wong, respectively. The cell line identities were confirmed by 
genotyping using short tandem repeat fingerprinting and were tested 
negative for mycoplasma with Mycoplasma PCR Detection Kit (ABM).
Dose-escalation experiments
To generate drug-induced resistant populations, 1 × 106 drug-naive 
cells were initially seeded in 150-mm plates. Twenty four hours after 
seeding, 1 μM of olaparib (Selleckchem, S1060) was added and cells 
were maintained under treatment until reaching confluency (>70%), 
thus characterizing resistance at this dose. Cells were collected (0.25% 
trypsin/EDTA for 5 min at 37 °C) and a fraction of the surviving popula-
tion (1 × 106 cells) was seeded again and treated with 2.5 μM olaparib 
until cells reached confluency. This process was repeated sequentially 
by doubling the drug concentrations until the cell populations were 
able to achieve confluency at 320 μM olaparib. The initial dose was 
determined by cell viability assays (see below), indicating a low start-
ing dose (giving less than 30% of the maximal response (IC30)). At each 
step, aliquots of the adapted populations were frozen (10% DMSO, 50% 
FBS, 40% medium) for further experiments. Using this approach, we 
generated nine drug-adapted lines for Kuramochi (T1–T320) and six 
adapted lines for Ovsaho and COV362 (T1–T40) plus the untreated 
controls (C). Similar dose-escalation experiments were performed 
for A375 cells treated with dabrafenib (Selleckchem, S2807), starting 
at 30 nM until resistant to 4 μM (8 drug-adapted lines plus untreated 
control), and for PC9 cells treated with osimertinib (Selleckchem, 
S7297), starting at 8 nM until resistant to 1.2 μM (8 drug-adapted lines 
plus untreated control).
To compare the resistant phenotypes generated by dose-escalation 
versus constant dose treatment, we repeated the dose escalation in 
triplicates for Kuramochi up to 10 μM, while initially drug-naive cells 
were maintained under treatment with the same doses (1, 2.5, 5 and 
10 μM) for the same amount of time in each step of the dose-escalation 
experiment. Medium with fresh drug was replenished every 3 days. 
Olaparib was added 24 h after plating, in the final concentrations from 
a stock solution of 50 mM dissolved in DMSO and maintained at −80 °C. 
Cell number was determined by trypan blue exclusion (Sigma-Aldrich) 
and counted on a Countess II cell counter (Life Technologies).
Cell viability assays
Viability experiments to test the resistance phenotypes (here defined 
as fitness) were performed using the CellTiter-Glo 2.0 Luminescent Cell 
Viability Assay (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. 
Before performing the viability assays, frozen aliquots of the resistant 
cells were thawed and cells were recovered without drug treatment for 
7–10 days. Cells were plated in 96-well plates at seeding density of 700 
to 800 cells per well. After 24 h, cells were treated with the drug with 
the same concentrations used in the dose-escalation experiments for 
6–9 days for the ovarian cancer cell lines, 4 days for A375 and 5 days 
for PC9 cells. For the olaparib and CB-839 (MCE, HY-12248) treatment 
combinations, dose–response curves were generated after 6-day treat-
ments on a range of olaparib doses (1 to 320 μM) with or without 50 nM 
of CB-839. This was performed on C, T5, T10, T40 and T320 adapted 
lines after sorting for CD44/CD24 populations as in Extended Data 
Fig. 5b. The effect of this drug combination was calculated using the 
Bliss synergy score41. DMSO without drug was used as a negative con-
trol to normalize the cell viability of drug treated wells. Luminescence 
was determined using the BioTek Cytation 5 Cell Imaging Multi-Mode 
Reader (Agilent). Cell viability analysis was performed on GraphPad 
Prism 9 and IC50 values were determined using a nonlinear regression 
curve fit. Pairwise significant differences between IC50 were assessed 
by Student’s two-tailed t-tests.
Cell cycle analysis
Cells were treated with 10 μM or 320 μM of olaparib 24 h after plating 
and cell cycle analysis was performed after 48 h of treatment using pro-
pidium iodide staining. Cells were fixed in cold 70% ethanol overnight 
at 4 °C, washed twice in PBS and resuspended in 1× PBS with 0.2 mg ml−1 
RNase A (Sigma-Aldrich) and 25 mg ml−1 propidium iodide (Biolegend). 
After 30 min incubation at room temperature in the dark, cells were 
analysed on a LSR II Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences). Quantification of 
cell cycle phases was performed on FlowJo software v.10.8.1 (FLOWJO) 
using the Watson model.
Drug-tolerant persister generation
Persister cells were generated similarly to those described in refs. 3,42 
by seeding 1 × 106 Kuramochi cells in 150 mm plates and treated with 
either 10 or 320 μM of olaparib for 9 days (greater than 90% inhibi-
tion concentration (>IC90)). Fresh medium with drug was added every 
3 days. For PC9 cells, treatment was 9 days with 1.2 μM of osimertinib. 
Cells that remained attached to the plates were considered persis­
ters. To confirm that the Kuramochi resistant phenotypes were due 
to adaptation rather than selection of pre-existing resistant clones, we 
performed two rounds of treatment-recovery of drug-naive cells. For 
this, we generated persisters in triplicates with 320 μM (the highest dose 
used in the escalation experiment) and cells were allowed to recover 
without drug treatment for 9 days prior to performing viability assays.
Apoptosis assay
To quantify apoptosis and cell viability of drug-tolerant persisters 
treated with different doses, we generated persister cells with either 10 
or 320 μM of olaparib as previously described. The adherent cells were 
collected (0.25% trypsin/EDTA for 5 min at 37 °C), stained with trypan 
blue and counted manually using haemocytometer or on a Countess II 
cell counter. Cells were washed twice in cold PBS and stained with DAPI 
(ThermoFisher) and Annexin V using the Annexin V Apoptosis Detec-
tion Kit (BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 
Stained cells were analysed on a LSR II Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences). 
Quantification of apoptotic and viable cells was performed on FlowJo 
software v.10.8.1 (Supplementary Fig. 2).
CD24 and CD44 cell staining and sorting
The untreated and resistant Kuramochi populations were collected and 
stained with DAPI (D1306, ThermoFisher), anti-CD24-PE (555428) and 
anti-CD44-APC (559942) (BD Biosciences). Populations were sorted 
on the basis of their CD24/CD44 profiles using a Sony SY3200 Highly 
Automated Parallel cell sorter and analysed with BD Facs Diva 8.0.2 and 
FlowJo v10.8.1. Sorted cells were used either for ATAC-seq or plating 
for cell viability experiments (Extended Data Fig. 5b).

Invasion assay was performed in 12 well plates with transparent PET 
membrane with 8-μm pore inserts (Falcon, 353182). Cells from C, T5, 
T320, C-Slug0 and C-Slug1 populations were plated in appropriate 
FBS-free medium containing 0.1% BSA (Sigma) on Transwell insert

Live-cell imaging

Lentiviral overexpression of SOX17 and Slug
Lentiviral vector for constitutive overexpression of SOX17 (TFORF2260) 
was a gift from F. Zhang (Addgene #141998). Lentiviral vector for induc-
ible expression of Slug (Addgene #82162) was Gateway-cloned under a 
doxycycline induced promoter on a pInducer20 backbone (Addgene 
#44012). JetPrime reagents and protocol (Polyplus) were followed for

adapted lines T5, T40 and T320 were infected with viral lysate before 
selection in the appropriate antibiotic (puromycin 2 μg ml−1 for SOX17; 
neomycin 2 μg ml−1 for Slug). The appropriate selection antibiotic was 
subsequently added to medium every 3 weeks in culture. To induce 
Slug, doxycycline was added to the cell medium at the indicated dose 
for 45 days prior to experiment initiation.
shRNA knockdown of SOX17
SOX17 inducible lentiviral small hairpin RNA (shRNA) gene set was pur-
chased from TransOmic Technologies (TLHSU2330). JetPrime reagents 
and protocol (Polyplus) were followed for transfection into HEK-293T 
packaging cells. Kuramochi Control cells were infected with one of 
three shRNA constructs or a non-targeting scrambled shRNA negative 
control construct by viral lysate before selection in the appropriate 
antibiotic (puromycin 1.5 μg ml−1). To induce SOX17 knockdown, doxy-
cycline (500 ng ml−1) was added to the cell medium for 3 days prior to 
drug exposure.
Generation of patient derived xenografts and drug treatments
All animal experiments were carried out under an approved animal 
protocol by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) 
of NYU Langone Grossmann School of Medicine. Immunodeficient 
six-week-old female NOD SCID gamma (NSG) (NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid 
Il2r gtm1Wjl/SzJ) mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory and 
allowed to acclimatize to housing conditions. Animals were housed 
in an specific-pathogen-free-grade animal facility at 25 ± 2 °C with a 
humidity of 40–70%. Besides, 12-h:12-h light periods were alternated 
between day and night, and these mice were given an unrestricted 
diet. Before the experiment, these mice were adaptively reared for 
more than three days. To generate high-grade serous ovarian cancer 
(HGSOC) PDX models of PARPi treatments, we used a previously charac-
terized BRCA2-mutated tumour derived from PDX models43 (ID: 66799). 
Approximately 1 × 106 cells in a 1:1 ratio with Matrigel Matrix (Corning) 
were subcutaneously injected with a 27-gauge needle (BD Micro-Fine) 
into the right lower mammary fat pad on the abdominal dorsal surface. 
Observation of animals including caliper measurements of tumour 
volume and body weight continued twice a week. Mice were euthanized 
with CO2 (confirmed by cervical dislocation) and tumours were excised 
for further procedures at sizes not exceeding the IACUC permitted 
measurements or volumes (up to 2,000 mm3 or up to 20 mm in one 
dimension). When tissue was available, a portion of the tumours was 
embedded in optimal cutting temperature and snap-frozen in a bath 
of isopentane chilled over liquid nitrogen. The tumour blocks were 
stored at −80 °C until use for staining. Tissue sections were mounted 
onto a slide at a thickness of 5–7 μm. Haematoxylin and eosin staining 
was performed and annotated by an expert gynaecologic pathologist.
PARPi dose–response. To study the transcriptional changes that 
occur upon different dose regimen, the BRCA2-mutant HGSOC cells 
were injected as previously described and mice were randomized 
into five groups after reaching 100–200 mm3 tumour volume. Treat-
ments were performed as follows: (1) Vehicle controls (10% dimeth-
ylacetamide, 5% Solutol HS15, 85% PBS) (n = 2); (2) dose 1 (D1) was 
0.13 mg kg−1 of talazoparib (Selleckchem, S7048) (n = 2); (3) dose 2 
(D2) was 0.23 mg kg−1 (n = 2); (4) dose 3 (D3) was 0.33 mg kg−1 (n = 2); and 
(5) dose 4 (D4) was 0.66 mg kg−1 (5 consecutive days a week, oral gav-
age). Mice were monitored by routine palpation and tumour sizes meas-
ured by caliper. Vehicle tumours were collected at sizes not exceeding 
the humane endpoint and talazoparib treated tumours were collected 
after 50–57 days of treatment.
PARPi resistance model. To establish a drug-resistance model, 
tumours were treated along three passages. In a first passage, when 
the PDX tumours reached 100–200 mm3 tumour volume, vehicle (10% 
dimethylacetamide, 5% Solutol HS15, 85% PBS) or talazoparib 
(0.0066 mg per mouse = 0.33 mg kg−1, oral gavage44) was adminis-
tered for 5 consecutive days a week. Talazoparib treatment caused 
tumour remission and treatment was interrupted. Tumour sizes were 
followed until reappearance and treatment was reintroduced. Vehicle 
and drug treated tumours that did not show further regression upon 
re-treatment—thus indicating resistance—were collected for scRNA-seq 
at sizes not exceeding the humane endpoint. A replicate tumour was 
implanted in a second passage and maintained under treatment upon 
tumour detection. The resulting tumour was implanted in a third passage, 
generating three replicates (two talazoparib treated and one vehicle).
Tumour dissociation for scRNA-seq
Upon arrival to the laboratory, tumours were minced with a razor blade 
and enzymatically dissociated in two steps. First, minced samples were 
incubated with 5 ml of digestion mix 1 (9 ml Advanced DMEM/F12, 1 ml 
Gentle Collagenase and 100 μl DNase I) at 37 °C for 30 min, centrifuged 
at 1,000 rpm for 4 min. After removing the supernatant, samples were 
incubated with 5 ml of digestion mix 2 (3 ml Advanced DMEM/F12, 2 ml 
Dispase, 5 ml TrypLE, 100 μl DNase I) at 37 °C for 15 min, placed on a 
70-μm cell strainer and washed with Advanced DMEM/F12 + 10% FBS. 
Samples were centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 4 min, incubated with 1 ml 
of ACK Lysing Buffer (ThermoFisher) for 4 min, washed with PBS + 10% 
FBS, resuspended in PBS, placed on a 35-μm cell strainer and viability 
was determined by trypan blue staining, which ranged from 65% to 
90%. Dissociated cells were collected for scRNA-seq immediately (resis­
tance experiment) or cryopreserved (dose–response experiment) by 
resuspending in freezing medium (50% FBS, 40% Advanced DMEM/
F12 and 10% DMSO) and stored at −80 °C. Batches of replicates were 
processed on the same day. Frozen samples were thawed, washed in 
medium, resuspended in PBS and the cell concentration adjusted for 
the 10× Genomics single-cell encapsulation protocol following library 
preparation and sequencing.

inDrop library preparation and sequencing
Cells from the Kuramochi dose-escalation experiment (C and T1–T320) 
were collected when they reached confluency at their respective dose 
treatments and collected for single-cell encapsulation and library 
preparation using the inDrop platform45 as previously described46. 
Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500 and reads were

hg38/GRGh38 human genome assembly and the Ensembl 93 transcrip-

10X Genomics library preparation and sequencing
We used the 10X Genomics workflow for scRNA-seq on cells collected 
for the comparison between Kuramochi persisters (P10 and P320) and 
adapted (T10 and T320) populations, as well as for Ovsaho, COV362, 
A375, PC9 dose-escalation experiments and for PDX data. Cell loading 
for droplet-based scRNA-seq and library preparation was performed 
using the 10X Genomics Chromium platform with the Chromium Single 
Cell 30 Library & Gel Bead Kit v3.1 following the manufacturer’s instruc-
tions. Libraries were diluted to 2 nM and 75 bp paired-end sequencing 
was performed using the Illumina NextSeq 500 and 150-200 million 
paired reads were generated for each library. Raw reads were processed 
with the CellRanger v3.1 or v.7.0.0 (10X genomics) pipeline (default 
parameters) with the hg38/GRCh38 genome and Ensembl 93 tran-
scriptome references. For PDX samples, reads were processed with the 
CellRanger multi-species detection and only cells assigned to ‘human’ 
were considered for further analysis.
Cell multiplexing for pooled scRNA-seq. To compare the expression 
among untreated (C), persister (P10 and P320; two replicates) and the 
adapted cells (T10 and T320), we used the cell hashing method47 to tag 
cells from different conditions based on the expression of surface mark-
ers (TotalSeqA, Hashtag oligonucleotides (HTOs) 1–5, Biolegend). Five 
samples from each condition were pooled into a single-cell suspension 
in equal amounts and loaded in the 10X Genomics Chromium Controller 
with Single Cell 3′ v3.1 system and libraries prepared according to the 
manufacturer’s protocol. For the hashtag oligos, 1 μl of HTO PCR additive 
primer was added to the 10X cDNA amplification step, and the superna-
tant from the 0.6× cDNA cleanup was kept and processed according to 
the Cell Hashing protocol, with 14 PCR cycles and a 1.2× cleanup after 
the PCR. The same procedure was applied to pool cells from Ovsaho (C 
and T5–T40; HTOs 1–5) and COV362 (C and T5–T40; HTOs 6–10) experi-
ments. Sample demultiplexing was performed using the CITE-seq-Count 
pipeline (v1.4.3; https://github.com/Hoohm/CITE-seq-Count) and Seu-
rat48. For the A375 and PC9 scRNA-seq, cell suspensions from each cor-
responding sample were labelled using the 10X Genomics 3′ CellPlex kit 
Set A according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were pooled in 
equal amounts and loaded in the 10X Genomics Chromium Controller. 
Demultiplexing of the corresponding CMOs was performed using Cell-
Ranger v.7.0.0 with 0.8 as assignment confidence.
ATAC-seq sample preparation and sequencing
ATAC-seq samples (C, P10, P320, T5–T320 and T320W) were prepared 
in duplicates using a modified version of the OMNI-ATAC protocol49. 
Briefly, cells grown in tissue culture under drug treatment with their 
respective adapted doses were trypsinized, stained for expression of 
CD44 and CD24 and sorted as previously described (C and T5–T320). To 
assess the stability of the chromatin states, T320 cells were grown for 
120 days without drug treatment, generating T320W. Together with its 
parental T320 (grown without drug for 10 days), cells were re-exposed 
to 320 μM. Cells from these four conditions (T320 and T320W on and 
off drug treatment) were collected for ATAC-seq in duplicates. Briefly, 
approximately 50,000 cells per condition were used for nuclei extrac-
tion using the ATAC-seq resuspension buffer as previously described49. 
Nuclei were resuspended in 50 μl of transposition mix (25 μl 2× TD 
buffer, 2.5 μl transposase (100 nM final), 16.5 μl PBS, 0.5 μl 1% digitonin, 
0.5 μl 10% Tween-20, and 5 μl water). Transposition reactions were 
incubated at 37 °C for 40 min in a thermomixer with shaking at 1,000 
rpm. Reactions were cleaned up with Zymo DNA Clean and Concen-
trator 5 columns. ATAC-seq libraries were amplified using Ad2_noMx 
(universal forward) and Ad2.x (indexed reverse). Libraries were diluted 
to 2 nM and 75 bp paired-end sequencing was performed using the 
Illumina NextSeq 500/550. Between 50 and 200 million paired reads 
were generated for each library.
Single-cell data analysis
Filtering. Raw gene expression matrices were TPM (transcripts per 
million)-normalized (scale factor of 104) and log2-transformed. For each 
cell, the number of transcripts, genes expressed, and the proportion 
of transcripts derived from mitochondrial and ribosomal genes were 
determined. For each dataset, cells with transcript number lower than 
500 or higher than 2× s.d., with less than 500 genes detected and with 
higher than 20% of transcripts corresponding to mitochondrial genes 
were excluded from further analysis. Genes expressed in less than 1% 
of the cells in each dataset were removed.
Cell clustering and differential gene expression. For all samples 
we observed that differences due to cell cycle gene expression were 
a major source of variation. To mitigate this effect for the interpreta-
tion of cell states, we used the Seurat function CellCycleScoring to 
assign cells to G1, S and G2/M phases. We then considered the G1 cells 
for further analyses. Dimensionality reduction, clustering and differ-
ential expression were performed using the Seurat R package v4.0.5 
and v2.3.2. To control for unwanted sources of variation, the number 
of unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and the fraction of mitochon-
drial and ribosomal UMIs were regressed out during data scaling. For 
the individual analysis of the Kuramochi datasets (C-T320) we used 
the DrImpute method (k parameter = 5:10)50 before clustering. Highly 
variable genes were identified using the Seurat FindVariableGenes 
function (default parameters) and these were used to compute the 
PCA following UMAP using the top five to ten principal components 
(cutoffs determined by the elbow method). Louvain clustering was 
performed using the FindClusters function using the selected pricipal 
components with resolution set to 0.3 to 0.6. For the analysis of PDX 
dose–response samples, we observed a batch effect driven mostly 
by one of the vehicle samples and we used the fastMNN method51 for 
correction of the aggregated datasets before clustering. Differential 
gene expression across clusters was performed using the FindMarkers 
function using the MAST test (logfc.threshold=0.25, min.pct=0.2, min.
cells.genes=10).
Defining cell states across resistant populations. To study cellular 
states across the Kuramochi resistant populations, we first assessed the 


===== PAGE 12 =====
differentially expressed genes among the cell clusters within each sam-
ple separately. The top 100 most differentially expressed genes (ranked 
by their adjusted P values) in each cluster were selected to generate a 
non-redundant list of genes (n = 1,511). These genes were used to build 
an average expression matrix of each cell cluster across all samples. 
A correlation matrix (Spearman’s correlation coefficients) across these 
average profiles was used for average linkage hierarchical clustering 
and the cell states were defined based on the cluster structure (k = 5).
Gene module identification. To identify gene expression programmes 
across the Kuramochi lines from C to T320, we implemented the app­
roach of Neftel et al.52, with some modifications. First, we aggregated 
all G1 cells (non-imputed dataset) from all conditions and performed 
an average linkage hierarchical clustering using the 1,000 most highly 
variable genes (defined by the FindVariableGenes function) on the 
centred dataset using one minus the Pearson correlation as the distance 
metric, as proposed previously52. To evaluate all possible clusters of 
cells that have a potential gene expression signature, we recovered all 
clusters excluding by size (less than 30 cells and no more than 80% of 
all cells) and by signal of differential expression. Each cluster of cells 
was evaluated for a signature of differential expression relative to the 
remaining cells using the FindMarkers function as described above. 
Clusters with a signature of differentially expressed genes were retained 
according to the following criteria: genes expressed >1.8 average fold 
change, adjusted P value < 0.001 and with more than 50 genes match-
ing these criteria. Gene signatures were then hierarchically clustered 
based on their Jaccard index and, for pairs with similarity above 0.75, 
the signature with the largest number of genes was considered. To 
integrate the gene signatures into gene modules, clusters with less 
than three signatures were excluded. The genes from all signatures 
that constitute a particular cluster were merged and ranked based on 
the ratio of their average expression in cells from a particular cluster 
relative to their average expression in cells from the other clusters. 
The gene module definition did not enforce mutual exclusivity, allow-
ing genes to be associated with multiple modules. To assess the sig-
nificance of gene overlap across modules and differentially expressed 
gene lists from other analyses, P values were computed with Fisher’s 
Exact tests implemented in the GeneOverlap v.1.26 R package. To assign 
EMT scores, cells were scored for the Hallmark EMT gene set (MSigDB) 
using the UCell 2.0.0 package in R. To assess the significance of overlap 
among genes in modules and genes in CNA regions, permutation tests 
(10,000) were performed using the regioneR 1.10.0 package53.
Identification of genes with dose–response patterns. Using the PDX 
datasets from the dose–response experiments, we identified genes 
with dose–response patterns by first performing pairwise differential 
expression across all treated conditions versus vehicles for each batch 
of replicates independently. Genes differentially expressed in both of 
the replicates were considered. A non-redundant list of these differ-
entially expressed genes was used to generate an average expression 
matrix across conditions. The samples were ordered from low to high 
doses (V, D1, D2, D3, D4) and the genes were clustered using the Mfuzz 
2.5.0 package54 setting the number of clusters to 7 and m parameter = 
1.8. Genes with membership score higher than 0.3 were included and 
those with similar profiles of decreasing or increasing expression rela-
tive to high (or low) doses in both replicates were aggregated.
Single-cell inferred CNAs
Large-scale chromosomal alterations from scRNA-seq datasets were 
inferred by the inferCNV v1.4.0 package. Untreated samples were used 
as a reference for CNA inference in the treated samples. The default 
options were used except for a cut-off of 0.1 for the minimum average 
read counts per gene and clustering by groups was set based on the 
cluster annotations determined from the transcriptome analyses. The 
output consisting of a denoised modified expression matrix as genes 
ordered by chromosomal location was used for heat map visualization 
considering 30% of the cells from each condition with ComplexHeat-
map 2.11.1 in R.
Gene set enrichment analysis
Gene set enrichment using the hallmark annotations (MSigDB), KEGG 
and Reactome pathways was performed using the R package fgsea 
v1.16. Differential expression between groups was determined using 
the MAST algorithm implemented in Seurat and the genes were ranked 
using the sign of the average log fold change multiplied by the −log10 
of P value. Ranked lists were used as inputs for the fgsea package with 
a minimum gene set size of 10 and a maximum of 500, and significance 
assessed by 10,000 permutations. Terms were considered significantly 
enriched at P values < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25 as suggested in the GSEA 
manual. For the analysis shown in Fig. 2b, the comparison was per-
formed across the adapted cells (that is, excluding untreated cells that 
belong to the module A annotation).
ATAC-seq data processing and analysis
Peak detection. Adapter sequences were removed using TrimGalore 
0.5.0 (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore) (-q 20, - --length 
30). Filtered reads were aligned to the human genome (hg38/GRCh38) 
with Bowtie255 v2.3.4.1 (--very-sensitive, -k 10, -X 2000). After removing 
reads mapping to mitochondrial DNA and duplicate reads removed 
with PicardTools 2.18.2 MarkDuplicates (http://broadinstitute.github.
io/picard/), only properly mapped pairs assigned by SAMTools 1.956 
were included for further analysis. To avoid peak detection bias due 
to sequencing depth differences, read depth across samples was 
downsampled with SAMTools to match the lowest depth. Peak call-
ing was performed with MACS2 v2.2.457 and blacklisted regions were 
excluded58. Reproducible peaks between replicates for each sample 
were determined using the IDR 2.0.3 framework (https://github.com/
nboley/idr) and peaks with high reproducibility (IDR score <0.05) were 
considered. A consensus peak set across all samples was constructed 
by merging the reproducible peaks from each sample using HOMER 
v4.159. Finally, a read count table for the consensus peaks was generated 
using deepTools 3.160.
Differential peak accessibility and motif analysis. The count 
table of consensus peaks was normalized and differentially accessible 
peaks were calculated using DESeq2 1.18.161. Differentially accessible 
peaks relative to the untreated sample (C) were considered as adjusted 
P < 0.001 and fold change ≥ 3. The top 2,000 most variable peaks were 
used for PCA. To visualize the peak signal (read counts) across samples, 
Zavit62 was used to sort the mean peak signal (between replicates) across 
samples. Motif enrichment analysis on the differentially accessible 
peaks was performed using HOMER’s findMotifsGenome for known 
transcription factor motifs. To assess the significance of the overlap 
between the genomic regions of the differentially accessible peaks and 
the regions that encompass CNAs (determined by WES), permutation 
tests (1,000 permutations) were performed using the regioneR pack-
age. To verify the influence of CNAs on the motif enrichment patterns, 
the motif analysis was repeated by removing the more or less differen-
tially accessible peaks that overlap with gained or deleted copy-number 
regions, respectively. To assess the stability of the chromatin states in 
T320 after drug removal (and re-exposure, Fig. 3f), the normalized peak 
signal (±1 kb of the peak centre) of the differentially accessible peaks 
for T320 cells (versus C) was determined using deepTools (bamCover-
age --binSize 1, --normalizeUsing RPGC; computeMatrix --binSize 1, 
--referencePoint center) and visualized with the plotHeatmap com-
mand. Differences in peak signal were assessed with Wilcoxon tests.
Whole-exome sequencing and analysis
Genomic DNA extraction for WES was performed using Qiagen Blood 
and Cell DNA kit (13323). The extracted DNA from representative

and processed according to the in-house pipeline. Briefly, 125 ng in

Hybridization and capture were performed using the relevant com-

Sequencing results were demultiplexed and converted to FASTQ 
format using Illumina bcl2fastq 2.2.0 software. The FASTQ files were 
processed using the Seq-N-Slide v21.1 pipeline (https://doi.org/10.5281/

variants with genomic context such as functional consequence on 
genes and identify presence in public variant databases gnomAD 3.0 
and COSMIC 90.
Metabolic CRISPR–Cas9 genetic screen
The human metabolic CRISPR–Cas9 knockout library was a gift from 
K. Birsoy24. Virus was produced by cotransfection of HEK293 cells with 
lentiviral library pool with packaging vectors (psPAX2 and VSV-G) 
using PEI Pro (PolyPlus). The titre of lentiviral supernatants was deter-
mined by infecting target cells with several amounts of virus in the 
presence of polybrene (8 μg ml−1; Millipore), counting the number of 
puromycin-resistant infected cells after 3 days of selection. Kuramochi 
C, T10 and T320 (CD44hiCD24low, sorted as in Extended Data Fig. 5b) 
cells were infected at a multiplicity of infection of ~0.3 and selected 
with puromycin (4 μg ml−1) 48 h after infection. An initial pool of cells 
was collected for genomic DNA extraction (initial time point). Remain-
ing cells were cultured for 10 population doublings either off (C, T10, 
T320) and on olaparib treatment (C in 1 μl and T320 in 40 μl), after 
which cells were collected for genomic DNA extraction (end time point). 
sgRNA inserts were PCR-amplified, gel purified, quantified with NEB-
Next Library Quantification Kit (E7630S) and sequenced on a Illumina 
NextSeq 500/550, generating between 20–40 million paired reads for 
each library.
Data analysis of the metabolic CRISPR–Cas9 genetic screen. 
Sequencing reads were mapped to the sgRNA library sequences with 
MAGeCK-count69 algorithm implemented in MAGeCK 0.5.8, resulting 
in 75–80% of mappability. The sgRNAs counts were used as inputs for 
the MAGeCK-MLE algorithm to calculate gene scores, which takes into 
account the differences in sgRNA abundance from the initial and the 
end points of the screen. Genes were considered depleted in a sample 
when having negative β scores, P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25, as suggested 
in the MAGeCK manual. To analyse gene depletions across samples 
(Fig. 5d), only genes with negative β scores were considered. Gene score 
differences between a sample and a reference were determined consid-
ering depleted genes (negative β scores in the sample) with FDR < 0.25 
and negative score differences higher than one standard deviation 
between the sample and the reference. The samples CD, T10 and T320 
were compared relative to C, and the T320D included comparisons with 
T320 and CD. The overlap among depleted genes in a sample relative to 
C were computed with Fisher’s Exact tests implemented in the GeneO-
verlap R package (Fig. 5c). For GSEA analysis, genes were ranked based 
on the negative score differences between a sample and a reference (for 
example, T320 versus C). Only genes with negative score differences 
were considered. GSEA was performed using the fgsea R package and 
significant terms were selected with P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25.
Immunoblotting
Samples were collected and lysed in the Laemmli buffer. Protein con-
centrations were determined using the Bio-Rad DC Protein Assay. 
Twenty milligrams of total protein was separated on a 4 to 12% bis-Tris 
gradient gel (Invitrogen) and then transferred onto PVDF membrane 
(Bio-Rad). Proteins were immunoblotted with the indicated antibodies. 
Goat anti-Rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) (H + L) horseradish peroxi-
dase (HRP) (31460) and Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) HRP (31430) were 
used as secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific). 
Immunoblots were visualized (Amersham Imager 600 or 680) using 
the Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Scientific, 32106), 
and quantification was performed using ImageJ software. Anti-beta 
actin antibody (Santa Cruz, sc-47778 or mAbcam 8226) was used as a 
loading control for all of the following staining, the NRF2 (Cell Signaling 
Technology (CST), 12721), ATF4 (CST, 11815), HO-1 (HMOX1, MA1-112), 
Slug (CST, 95855) and SOX17 (CST, 8177). Full scans are provided in 
Supplementary Figs. 6–8 and 10.
Targeted metabolomics profiling
Sample processing. Kuramochi cells (C and T320) were cultured until 
~80% confluence either on 320 μM of olaparib for 24 h or off treatment 
in triplicates per condition. Cell pellets were collected and frozen with 
liquid nitrogen for submission to the NYU Metabolomics Core Resource 
Laboratory. Samples were analysed by liquid chromatography–mass 
spectrometry to detect and quantify known peaks. A metabolite extrac-
tion was carried out on each sample based on a previously described 
method70. The LC column was a MilliporeTM ZIC-pHILIC (2.1 × 150 mm, 
5 μm) coupled to a Dionex Ultimate 3000TM system and the column 
oven temperature was set to 25 °C for the gradient elution. A flow rate 
of 100 μl min−1 was used with the following buffers: 10 mM ammonium 
carbonate in water, pH 9.0 (buffer A) and neat acetonitrile (buffer B). 
The gradient profile was as follows: 80–20% B (0–30 min), 20–80% B 
(30–31 min), 80–80% B (31–42 min). Injection volume was set to 2 μl 
for all analyses (42 min total run time per injection). Mass spectrom-
etry analyses were carried out by coupling the liquid chromatography 
system to a Thermo Q Exactive HFTM mass spectrometer operating in 
heated electrospray ionization mode. Method duration was 30 min with 
a polarity switching data-dependent top 5 method for both positive and 
negative modes. Spray voltage for both positive and negative modes 
was 3.5 kV and capillary temperature was set to 320oC with a sheath gas 
rate of 35, aux gas of 10, and max spray current of 100 μA. The full mass 
spectrometry scan for both polarities utilized 120,000 resolution with 
an AGC target of 3 × 106 and a maximum IT of 100 ms, and the scan range 
was from 67–1,000 m/z. Tandem mass spectra for both positive and 
negative mode used a resolution of 15,000, AGC target of 105, maximum 
IT of 50 ms, isolation window of 0.4 m/z, isolation offset of 0.1 m/z, 
fixed first mass of 50 m/z, and 3-way multiplexed normalized collision 
energies (nCE) of 10, 35, 80. The minimum AGC target was 104 with an 
intensity threshold of 2 × 105. All data were acquired in profile mode.
Data analysis. The resulting ThermoTM RAW files were converted to 
mzXML format using ReAdW.exe version 4.3.1 to enable peak detec-
tion and quantification. The centroided data were searched using an 
in-house Python script, Mighty_skeleton version 0.0.2 and peak heights 
were extracted from the mzXML files based on a previously established 
library of metabolite retention times and accurate masses adapted 
from the Whitehead Institute71, and verified with authentic standards 
and/or high resolution MS/MS spectral manually curated against the 
NIST14MS/MS72 and METLIN (2017)73 tandem mass spectral libraries. 
Metabolite peaks were extracted based on the theoretical m/z of the 
expected ion type—for example, [M + H]+, with a ±5 ppm tolerance, and 
a ±7.5 s peak apex retention time tolerance within an initial retention 
time search window of ±0.5 min across the study samples. The resulting 
data matrix of metabolite intensities for all samples and blank controls 


===== PAGE 14 =====
was processed with an in-house statistical pipeline, Metabolyze version 
1.0, and final peak detection was calculated based on a signal to noise 
ratio (S/N) of 3× compared to blank controls, with a floor of 10,000 
(arbitrary units). For samples where the peak intensity was lower than 
the blank threshold, metabolites were annotated as not detected, and 
the threshold value was imputed for any statistical comparisons to 
enable an estimate of the fold change as applicable. The resulting blank 
corrected data matrix was then used for all group-wise comparisons, 
and t-tests were performed with the Python SciPy (1.1.0) library to test 
for differences and generate statistics for downstream analyses. Any 
metabolite with P < 0.05 was considered significantly regulated (up 
or down). Heat maps were generated with hierarchical clustering per-
formed on the matrix values utilizing the R library ComplexHeatmap.

exposure and treatment duration. a, Generation of drug-tolerant persisters

at increased concentrations (T10, T40, T160, respectively) (see Fig. 1a), generating 
the extended T5-E, T10-E and T80-E populations (three independent replicates). 
Prolonged exposure at constant doses did not generate similarly resistant 
populations for the T5 and T20 extended treatments when compared to their 
dose-escalated counterparts (T10 and T40, respectively). The extended 
treatments did however lead to higher resistance relative to their parental

indicate that both dose and time influence the fitness outcomes along the 
adaptive path. Cell viability was measured by CellTiter Glo 2.0 (each biological

higher doses (5 and 10 μM). The continuous long-term 10 μM treatment led to
